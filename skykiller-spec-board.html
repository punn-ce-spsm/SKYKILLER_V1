Demonstrator — not a fielded system. Receive-only by construction · no transmit path is built Rev D supersedes rev A–C: the platform is airborne, not a ground mast

This is the demo-scale build of a counter-UAS system on the SkyLock pattern: many sensors feeding one fused air picture, an operator console that owns the decision, and a graduated ladder of effects. The whole detect–track–identify chain is real and runs on real hardware. The effect stage is simulated end to end — the system computes, displays, and logs an engagement, and transmits nothing.

That split is not a shortcut, it is the design. Jamming and GNSS interference are unlawful without specific authority almost everywhere, and the interesting engineering — multi-sensor fusion, track continuity, classification, the rules-of-engagement gate — all lives upstream of the transmitter. Building the sensing and decision half honestly gets you a system that is legal to run on a Saturday in a field, and that has a real transmitter-shaped hole waiting for it if authority is ever granted.

What changed in rev D, and why the rest of the sheet moved with it. Revisions A–C specified a single ground mast. That was the wrong shape for the problem the customer actually has. Their existing ground cameras are not short of sensitivity; they are short of line of sight — a 20 m treeline 200 m out hides everything below 88 m of altitude at 1 km, and the drones that matter fly at 50. No lens fixes that. So the sensor head goes up on a tether, and because a tether cannot fly a parallax baseline, there are two of them. Altitude is the product; the second post is what turns bearings into positions. §2, §3, §7, §8 and §10 are rewritten around that.

Two of the numbers here are now measured rather than estimated, and they are marked where they appear. The visual lane and the whole fusion, identification and decision stack are built and tested — 203 tests, and a scripted demonstration that runs on a laptop with no hardware at all. Everything involving radio, acoustics, an airframe or a tether is still specification.

Every unmeasured number on this sheet is a demo-scale estimate for the specific parts listed in §8, not a vendor specification. Ranges in particular vary by a factor of three with antenna, terrain, and ambient RF.

1

Scope — what is real, what is simulated, what is never run

truth table

Read this before anything else on the sheet. A counter-drone demo that blurs these columns is a liability; one that states them plainly can be shown to anyone.

Real

Works on an uncooperative target. Bold = built and tested today; the rest is specified

  • EO detection, classification and visual track — measured, §3
  • Pixel → bearing through a full camera pose, correct for a camera that looks down and off-axis
  • Two-post triangulation to an ENU position with a covariance ellipse
  • Cross-site association — which of post A’s contacts is which of post B’s, and a flag when the geometry cannot say
  • Kinematic tracking — constant-velocity Kalman filter in local ENU, with track merging
  • Identification by cooperation — correlation against a feed only friendlies produce
  • ROE state machine — two human acts, continuously revoked
  • Terrain line-of-sight model — what a given mast height can and cannot see
  • RF energy detection and coarse bearing on 2.4 / 5 GHz — passive spectral scan
  • Remote ID decode (Wi-Fi NAN + Bluetooth 5 Long Range)
  • Acoustic rotor-signature detection and bearing
  • Operator console and audit log
  • CoT export to a TAK server / ATAK end user device

Simulated

Real message contracts and real timing, synthetic outcome

  • All three effect tiers — issued, timed, logged, never radiated
  • The cooperative friendly feed — real message contract, synthetic source, so any real feed plugs in unchanged
  • Scenario generation — synthetic aircraft on known trajectories driving the real pipeline, which is how accuracy is measured against truth rather than asserted
  • Radar plots, injected onto the live bus by a track generator
  • The target’s post-effect behaviour (return-to-home, descent)
  • The net interceptor’s intercept solution and flight path
  • Effect-assessment feedback — “did the target respond?”

Never run

Not built, and not reachable from any code path in the system

  • Any transmission on a drone control band
  • Any GNSS spoofing or GNSS denial
  • Kinetic launch against an aircraft that has not consented
  • Any effect issued without a logged human authorisation
  • Operation against a third party’s aircraft
The guarantee, stated precisely. No wideband transmitter and no RF amplifier is in the build. The SDRs are receive-only silicon. The three parts that can transmit — two Wi-Fi cards and an ESP32 — are commodity, low-power, narrowband radios run strictly in passive modes: monitor, NAN listen, BLE scan. None of them can produce the wideband power a denial effect requires, and no effect code path touches a radio at all. So the honest claim is not “nothing here can radiate” — it is that nothing here can perform T1 or T2, by construction rather than by discipline.

System architecture

fig. 1

Four bands, left to right, in the order the information travels: sense, fuse, decide, hand off. Every lane on every post publishes the same Detection message onto one bus and knows nothing about the lanes beside it. A sensor does not know where it is standing; src names the post and fusion looks the surveyed position up. That is what lets a second post be added without a line of lane code changing.

The two structural changes from rev A sit in the middle band. Association now happens across sites before anything is fused — with two aircraft in the sky there are four candidate pairings and two of them are wrong, and a wrong pairing intersects perfectly well, producing a confident position where nothing is flying. And identity is decided by cooperation, not by signature: both sides fly the same airframes on the same bands, so nothing about a contact separates friend from foe. What separates them is that our aircraft report themselves and theirs do not.

Why the bus matters more than any single sensor. Each lane below is individually mediocre and individually defeatable — and each is blind to a band or a condition the others cover. That is the honest position at this budget. The value is in the union: an attacker who goes radio-silent to beat L1 still makes rotor noise for L3, and an attacker who flies at night to beat L2 still has to emit or be heard. Fusion is what turns four weak sensors into one that is hard to walk past.

The measured L2 numbers carry one trap that will bite any reimplementation. Detection does not degrade gracefully as you shrink the input — it falls off a cliff, because a distant drone is the pixels that downscaling throws away. On a 1280-wide capture of a real quadcopter: at imgsz 640 the detector found the target in 0 of 60 frames at any confidence down to 0.01; at 800, five; at 960, twenty-nine; at 1280, all sixty. The frame rate moves the other way — 17.8 fps at 640 against 4.7 at 1280 — so the tempting optimisation is exactly the one that blinds the lane. Infer at the size you trained at, and treat any frame-rate figure quoted without an imgsz beside it as meaningless.
Remote ID is the highest-value lane per dollar, and the easiest to over-trust. Fifty dollars of receiver hands you a cooperative aircraft’s serial number, its position, and where its pilot is standing — a capability that costs six figures to approximate any other way. It is also purely a courtesy protocol: it tells you about every drone except the one flown by someone who does not want to be told about. Treat an L1b identity as strong evidence and its absence as no evidence at all.

Kill chain and latency budget

fig. 2

Drawn to scale on a single time axis, because the scale is the argument. The machine half of the chain fits in about a third of a second; the human decision is five to fifteen times longer than everything else combined. That is not a performance problem to be optimised away — it is the safety property the whole system exists to protect.

One measured number does not fit the budget above, and it is worth saying so on the sheet. FIG. 2 draws the detector at 60 ms. The lane as built runs at 213 ms per frame — YOLO11x at imgsz 1280 on Apple MPS, which is 4.7 fps. That is adequate for a hovering or walking-pace target and marginal above roughly 10 m/s, and it is the heavy x variant only because it is the only public drone-trained weight. Fine-tuning an n or s model is the available fix and is not blocking. The wider point stands unchanged: the machine half of the chain is still an order of magnitude faster than the operator half, so shaving it buys nothing a person can perceive.
5

Effector ladder

fig. 3

Three tiers of escalating force, each with a real message contract, a real timing model, and a simulated outcome. The demonstrator executes the ladder in full — arms, authorises, escalates, logs — and radiates nothing at any tier.

The inversion in that last column is the real finding. Tier 1 is a weekend of software and a $300 transmitter, and firing it once is a federal offence in most of the world. Tier 3 is a hard robotics problem — and it is the tier a private builder can lawfully develop, test and fly, and the only one that touches an autonomous target with no radio link to break. If this project ever grows past a demonstrator, the effect work belongs in T3, and T1–T2 stay simulated permanently unless someone arrives with an authority document.

Operator console

fig. 4

The console is where the two seconds of §4 get spent, so it is designed around one question: can a person decide correctly, from a cold start, in the time the track gives them? Everything on the threat card exists to answer that — what it is, how sure we are, how it got that sure, where its pilot is standing, and how long until it matters.

This section is a design, not a build. The decision logic behind it — the ROE state machine, the two-act gate, the continuous revocation — is built and tested. What is drawn here is the surface in front of that logic, and it is scheduled at P3. Read the figure as a specification for what the operator must be shown, not as a screenshot of something that runs.

Line of sight — why the observer is airborne

fig. 5

This is the section rev D exists for, and it contains no sensor engineering at all. The customer’s ground cameras are not short of sensitivity. They are short of an unobstructed line to the target, and that is a geometry problem with exactly one lever: height.

A sight line from a camera at height hc grazing a crest of height ho at distance do arrives at range R already at altitude hc + (ho − hc) · R / do. Everything below it is invisible. Put real numbers in and the result is worse than most people expect:

Obstruction, seen from a 3 m ground camera Lowest visible altitude @ 1 km @ 2 km
20 m treeline at 200 m88 m173 m
20 m treeline at 500 m37 m71 m
60 m ridge at 1 km60 m117 m

A quadcopter crossing a border at 50 m AGL is beneath every one of those figures at 1 km. It is not that the camera sees it poorly — there is no photon path. No lens, sensor, or detector fixes this. Raising the same camera to 200 m does, immediately and completely.

Measured, not asserted — and the harness is built so it can fail. Running the same inbound target past a 3 m ground pair and a 200 m tethered pair, identical software and identical bearing noise, behind the 20 m treeline above: the ground pair first holds the track at 63.2 s and 563 m, giving 4.4 s of warning before the target reaches a 500 m envelope. The tethered pair holds it from 0.0 s at 1504 m — 67.6 s. Fifteen times the warning, from altitude alone.

Two ways of reading that are wrong, and the harness guards against both. Envelope timings are taken from truth, never from the system’s own estimate, so a bad fix cannot flatter the warning figure — wreck the sensing to 5° of bearing noise and the entry time does not move while the accuracy collapses. And the tethered pair’s median error over the whole run is worse (11.9 m against 3.3 m), because it holds the target for 95 s of mostly long range where a bearings-only fix is honestly poor, while the ground camera only ever sees it inside 600 m. At the envelope edge — the one moment an effector cares about — the tethered pair is better: 1.7 m against 4.5 m.

Remove the treeline and the advantage vanishes entirely. The case rests on terrain, and the demonstration ships with a switch that turns the terrain off, because a claim you cannot attack is not worth making.
Two siting numbers, both measured, both costing warning time rather than correctness.
Posts 200 m apart, not 100. The baseline sets usable range — the crossing angle shrinks as the target recedes, and at 100 m the solver holds every frame at 1 km but only 73% at 2.5 km. It also halves position error at the ranges that matter.
Observers above the altitude band they watch. This one is not obvious. Two targets that subtend the same elevation from a post put every ray in one horizontal plane, and then the true and crossed cross-site pairings intersect equally well — the assignment is a coin toss and no software resolves it. Raising the masts separates targets at different ranges in elevation, because the near one gains depression angle faster than the far one. Measured over twelve runs: masts at 120 m gave 0.19° of separation and a hostile declared at 15.4 s; masts at 200 m gave 3.34° and 3.0 s. Twelve seconds is 185 m of standoff at 15 m/s.

Demo site and scripted profile

fig. 6

Drawn to scale on a 600 × 600 m private field. The target flies a fixed three-leg profile chosen so that each leg brings a new lane online — the audience watches the track get better as it gets closer, which is the whole point of building four sensors.

This is the plan view; §7 is the elevation, and the elevation is where the argument lives. A ring drawn on a map says nothing about whether there is an optical path to the aircraft inside it, and on real border terrain there usually is not. Read the two figures together: this one sizes the field and the profile, FIG. 5 says what any of it can actually see.

Two posts, 200 m apart, both tethered. Post A is the ENU frame origin and post B is surveyed against it — a one-time measurement, and the tether is most of why it stays true: a tethered observer hangs above a fixed mast, so its position is known rather than estimated every frame. A free-flying observer would need its own navigation solution and its own error budget stacked on top of the bearing error, and the tether deletes that entire problem.

Build plan

B1–B2 done · P1–P4 open

Each phase ends on a single falsifiable test. If the exit criterion does not pass, the phase is not done — the failure this schedule exists to avoid is four half-working sensor lanes and no fused track.

The order inverted, and that was the right call. Rev A sequenced hardware first and fusion at P2. In practice the fusion, identification and decision stack was built first, against synthetic scenarios driving the real pipeline — which meant the two defects that mattered were found on a laptop rather than in a field. Neither was visible in unit tests: with two aircraft in the sky nothing was pairing post A’s contacts with post B’s, and the resulting ghosts were reaching HOSTILE. Both would have cost a day of field time each to even observe.

B1done

Visual lane built

The L2 detect–track–bearing chain: YOLO11 detection, BoT-SORT tracking, pixel → bearing through a full camera pose, and the Detection contract on the bus. Runs on a laptop webcam or a file. An identity filter that gates emission on a known target — faces indoors today, Remote ID serials later, same code.

Exit criterionVerified on synthetic imagery: 60 of 60 frames detected, one stable track id, azimuth monotonic through boresight. The flight half is not done — a Mini 4 Pro holding one id for 30 s needs registration and a site first.
B2done

Fusion, identification, decision built

Cross-site association, two-post triangulation with covariance, the Kalman air picture with track merging, cooperative IFF, the ROE gate, the terrain model, and a scenario harness that drives all of it against known truth. 203 tests.

Exit criterionAll four met. Known targets recover to < 1 m from exact bearings and the reported covariance matches the measured spread. A track matching the friendly feed reads FRIENDLY, one that does not reads HOSTILE, and killing the feed flips the first to UNKNOWN rather than to either certainty. One ingress raises exactly one ARM prompt. The masking model reproduces §7’s table from first principles.
P1~3 wk

Sensor hardware, one post

L1a RF energy with two-antenna bearing, L1b Remote ID over Wi-Fi NAN and Bluetooth 5 Long Range, L3 acoustic. Blocked on procurement lead time, not on difficulty — and L1b is blocked on whether the target aircraft broadcasts at all, which is a ten-minute test nobody has run yet.

Exit criterionA target powered up at 200 m yields its serial, its position and its operator’s position on the bus within 3 s of spin-up.
P2~3 wk

Second post, and the tether

Airframe, tether, power and fibre, and the survey that fixes both posts in one ENU frame. The software downstream does not change — it has been running against two posts since B2 — so this phase is entirely mechanical and electrical.

Exit criterionTwo surveyed posts hold a real aircraft at 500 m and report a position whose error against a surveyed waypoint lies inside the covariance the system itself claims. A fix that is accurate but overconfident fails this test.
P3~2 wk

Console and interop

The operator console of §6 against live tracks, the append-only audit log, and CoT export to a TAK server. The ROE state machine behind it is already built and tested; this is the surface in front of it.

Exit criterionAn operator ARMs and authorises T1 on a live track; the request lands in the audit log with full sensor provenance, nothing radiates, and the same track appears as a CoT marker in ATAK on a second machine.
P4~1 wk

Demonstration

Three things the customer has to see, and only the first is novel.

Exit criterionAltitude advantage side by side — a low camera behind an obstruction holds no track while the elevated pair does, same target, same moment. Early warning on a clock — first detection range, envelope entry, dwell seconds. Accuracy against truth — the target flown through surveyed points, reported versus actual.
P5optional

Hard-kill, if it is ever worth it

The net interceptor: airframe, launcher, and closing the loop from the fused track to a flown MAVLink intercept. A separate project in scale, and the only effector this build could lawfully fire.

Exit criterionA net intercept of a consenting, instrumented target over a cleared impact area, with a safety pilot able to abort at any point.

Standing constraints

read before every run

These are not disclaimers appended to the end of the sheet; they are the constraints the architecture was drawn around, which is why the transmitter-shaped hole in §2 is empty.

No effect-capable transmitter exists

The SDRs are receive-only silicon. The Wi-Fi cards and the ESP32 are transmit-capable radios, but they are narrowband, low-power, and run only in passive receive modes — none can produce a denial effect, and no effect code path opens a radio. Keep it that way: if a wideband transmit-capable SDR (HackRF, Pluto, bladeRF) or any RF amplifier ever enters the build, this entire document stops applying.

Jamming and GNSS interference

Unlawful without specific authority in effectively every jurisdiction. In the United States, counter-UAS mitigation authority sits with a short statutory list of federal agencies and does not extend to private parties or most law enforcement. Check your own jurisdiction before assuming anything here transfers; assume it does not.

Reception is not interception

Listening to Remote ID is receiving a beacon a drone broadcasts deliberately. Passively noting that energy exists in a band is generally fine too. Decoding the content of someone’s control or video link is a different act with a different legal character — the build detects and characterises signals, it does not demodulate payloads.

Remote ID captures personal data

An L1b hit contains a pilot’s live physical location. Treat those records the way you would treat any location data about an identifiable person: retain them for the run, then discard. Do not publish console screenshots containing a real operator position — the one in FIG. 4 is invented.

Flight operations

Private land with written permission, a safety pilot on the sticks for the whole run, visual line of sight at all times, airspace checked on the day, and a declared safe box. The target is your own aircraft and no one else’s — pointing any part of this at a third party’s drone is out of scope at every phase.

Identity data is biometric data

The identity filter that gates emission on a known target stores a face embedding when it is tested indoors, and that is biometric data about a real person. It stays on the machine that made it, the directory holding it is excluded from version control, and deleting the file revokes it. The source photograph is the same category and was excluded only after it had already been committed once — which is the useful lesson: the derived artefact was protected and the obvious original was not. Enrol another person only with their say-so.

Say what it is

When showing this, lead with §1. A demonstrator described honestly as detect-and-decide with simulated effects is genuinely impressive engineering. The same demonstrator described as a working counter-drone weapon is a claim you cannot support and should not make. The same discipline applies to the numbers: §7’s figures are measured and the sheet says which, and the demonstration ships with the switch that turns its own advantage off.
